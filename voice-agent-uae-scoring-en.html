What manual call review cannot do

Coverage

A few percent get heard

A supervisor gets through a handful of calls a day. Everything else goes to the archive unreviewed — along with the lost deals and the compliance breaches inside it.

Consistency

Scores don't add up

Two supervisors grade the same conversation differently. There is no common scale to compare campaigns, languages or script versions against each other.

Latency

Feedback arrives late

A flaw in the script survives for weeks before anyone spots it. The entire lead flow passes through it in the meantime.

Measurementthe score is issued by an AI agent

Who grades the call, and how

Grading is done by a separate AI agent — not by a person, and not by the same model that held the conversation. It runs automatically after every call. No sampling, no queue, no supervisor in the loop.

  • 01A different model from the one on the phone. The evaluating agent runs on a model distinct from the dialogue agent. Otherwise the system would be marking its own homework, and would always be pleased with itself.
  • 02Not everything is judged by AI. Response latency, talk ratio, interruptions and recognition confidence are computed in code from telemetry — that is measurement, not opinion. The evaluating agent is used only where meaning has to be interpreted.
  • 03Two passes, not one. First, extraction: what the agent claimed, what it asked, what the customer confirmed. Then scoring against a rubric applied to that structure. Score variance drops sharply compared with handing a model a raw transcript and asking for a number.
  • 04Rubrics with anchors. Every point on every scale has a worked example from a real call attached to it. Without anchors, any model drifts toward the middle of the range.
  • 05Every point is traceable. Each deduction arrives with the quote and turn number it came from. Your broker and your compliance officer see not "73", but exactly where it was lost.
Pass 1 · Extraction

The conversation becomes structure

Verifiable elements are pulled out of the transcript: claims about price and handover, the moment of disclosure, captured qualification data, the response to a refusal. The output is JSON, not a summary.

Pass 2 · Scoring

The rubric is applied to that structure

The evaluator grades the extracted elements on a fixed scale and reconciles every factual claim against what the inventory service actually returned. That is a comparison, not a judgement about how truthful the call sounded.

14:22 Agent: …studios in that tower start at AED 720,000, with handover in Q4 next year.
14:29 Customer: And what kind of return does that give me?
14:31 Agent: Average rental yield in that community last year was around 7%. I can't guarantee a return — that's the market. Evaluator output: C1 pass — price and handover matched to inventory · D5 pass — no guaranteed-return claim · F2 = 3 / 4 — objection handled, but not converted into a next step

How the 100 points are allocated

Metrics sit in six blocks with fixed weights. Each block can earn up to its weight in points; the weighted sum is the raw score, and hard gates apply a ceiling on top of it. Drag inside a block to change its result, and switch on a gate to see the ceiling take effect.

Target ranges, not maximums

Most indicators are not "more is better". An agent holding 85% of the airtime sells badly. One holding 15% sells badly too. Those metrics are normalised against a target band rather than scaled linearly.

Gates sit outside the weights

A regulatory breach must not be diluted by a weight and offset by an excellent pitch. Compliance is worth 20 points — and it also caps the entire call.

Grounding carries real weight

Price, handover date and payment plan are checked line by line against the inventory service. In off-plan sales this is the block that protects the brokerage, not just the score.

Assurancewhy the score can be trusted

The metric is itself audited

An automated score is only worth having if it agrees with human judgement and predicts revenue. Both are measured, and both are reported.

Gold set

A stratified sample of calls across language, outcome and duration is annotated by two independent reviewers, with a third resolving disagreements. That set is the benchmark the evaluator is tested against.

Agreement

Inter-annotator agreement and evaluator-to-human correlation are tracked per block. Weak agreement means the rubric gets rewritten — not that the model gets nudged.

Weights from outcomes

Initial weights are set by domain experts, then refined by regressing sub-scores against booked viewings and closed deals, with smoothing applied to low-volume segments.

Does it predict revenue?

The decisive test: conversion in the top scoring quintile versus the bottom. If there is no gap, the metric is measuring politeness rather than selling, and the weights are rebuilt.

Resistant to gaming

Regularly checked that the score cannot be raised cheaply — for instance by reciting the compliance block and ending the call early.

Drift monitoring

Sub-score distributions are tracked weekly. A shift in the median with an unchanged script points to degraded recognition or a change in lead mix.

Where this approach has limits

  • CQS scores the call, not the lead. A flawless conversation with an irrelevant contact earns a high score and produces nothing — so the score is always read alongside a breakdown by lead source.
  • Factual checking is only as reliable as the inventory feed. A stale price at the source becomes an honest error the metric will not catch.
  • Replacing the evaluating model requires re-running the gold set before the scores remain comparable.
  • Regulatory thresholds are configuration for a given jurisdiction and launch date, and remain subject to your legal review.
